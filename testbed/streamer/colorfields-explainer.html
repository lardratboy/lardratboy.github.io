Colour Fields

The DAG holds shape and nothing else. A node's id says which voxels are there, and two places with the same voxels are the same node, whatever colour they are. So colour lives beside the DAG, as a field: a function from a point to a colour, asked by the chunk job once for every cube it's about to draw. This page follows a colour from the world's name for it to the pixel, one step a section, with the real code running.

01

The whole road

Three things are easy to mix up, so here they are once, apart:

  • A descriptor is a small object that names a colour: { kind: 'shape', name: 'terrain', D: 8 }. It's what a world carries, what crosses to a worker and what goes in a file. It's data, because a function can't be posted to a worker.
  • A field is the function a descriptor turns into: colour(x, y, z, level), returning 8-bit RGB packed in one integer. Each side (a worker, the main-thread fallback, Node) makes its own from the descriptor with colourOf.
  • A word is what the job writes for one drawn cube: 32 bits, 6 of them per channel. It's the only form of colour the GPU ever sees.

The job is where a field becomes words. For each cube a chunk will draw, cellAttr asks the field and packs the answer. Everything before that is about getting the right field to the job. Everything after is about carrying words to a pixel.

02

What a field is

The whole contract is one line, :

colour(x, y, z, level)  →  r | g << 8 | b << 16      // 8-bit channels, one integer, no allocation
colour.mean(level, x, y, z, out)  →  out[0..2]        // optional: only a baked field has it (§6)
  • The point is in voxel units, and a voxel's centre is at i + 0.5. Voxel 12 spans [12, 13), so its colour is asked at 12.5. A coarse sample is asked at the centre of its sub-cell: corner + side / 2, which for any side above 1 is a whole number, the corner where eight voxels meet.
  • It's defined everywhere, not only inside the shape. The job only asks at occupied cells, but a coarse sample's centre can sit in air even when its cell holds voxels (§5). So every named field answers for every point of the cube, and the kit's and the lattice's return grey between their pieces.
  • It's a pure function of the point. No state, no order, no neighbours. That's what lets every worker rebuild it alone and get the same bytes, and what lets a chunk's words depend on that chunk only.
  • It returns 8 bits a channel, though a word keeps 6. A coarse mean is summed from these in floats, and the 6-bit rounding happens once at the end (§3).
  • The fourth argument is the sample's level. The point is the centre of a level-level cell: 0 at lod 0, the walk's floor level below it. Every named field ignores it. The scene field needs it (§8).

"Everywhere" asks the field at every voxel centre of the slice and dims the answers where there's no voxel. Nothing in the engine draws those: they're what a coarse sample can land on.

Look at the kit and the lattice in "everywhere": between the pieces the field is flat grey. Look at terrain: its bands carry on up into the sky. Both are the same promise kept two ways. A coarse sample that lands in air gets something sensible, and its weight decides how much that matters.

03

The word

There are two packings, and they're easy to confuse because both put red in the low byte:

whatlayoutmade byread by
a field's answer8 bits a channel: r | g << 8 | b << 16rgb8the job (cellAttr), files' baked words
an attribute word6 bits a channel, in the high 6 bits of each colour byte: shifts 2, 10, 18packAttr, attrOf8attrRGB (JS) and GLSL_ATTR (shader), only

A word read as four RGBA8 bytes is a colour you can look at: each colour byte is the 6-bit value times 4. That's on purpose (Q14): an image codec that predicts per channel sees one channel per byte. The 14 bits left over (the low 2 of each colour byte, and all of the fourth byte) are reserved: written 0, ignored on read, so every word made today stays valid when something is put there.

red, bits 2–7green, bits 10–15 blue, bits 18–23reserved, written 0

All 256 values of a channel, and the 64 they land on

  • to6 is round-to-nearest of v · 63 / 255. 0 goes to 0 and 255 to 63, so black and white survive. It takes a float, because a coarse mean is one.
  • The rounding is the only loss, and it happens once per word. A step is 255 / 63 ≈ 4.05 in 8-bit units, so the worst error is half that, about 2.02. Averaging words that were already rounded would round again at every lod. So means are taken in 8-bit floats and rounded at the end (decision 6).
  • The shader divides by 63, not 64. attrColour(w) = vec3(attrRGB(w)) / 63.0, so 63 is exactly 1.0. The transform-feedback probe in chunk-viewer.html reads every one of the 218 colours back through GLSL_ATTR and compares with attrRGB, exactly.
  • A new field in the reserved bits must bring its own coarse reduction. Colour's is the count-weighted mean. A material id couldn't use a mean: 3 and 5 don't average to 4. That's the rule that keeps the 14 bits from being spent carelessly (attributes-q13.md §3).

05
From field to word: cellAttr


  
One function turns a field into a word, and it has three branches
  ():

  
if (colour.mean)   // a baked field: ask it for the cell's mean, at every lod, lod 0 included (§6)
if (lod === 0)     // a voxel: one sample at its centre, colour(x + 0.5, y + 0.5, z + 0.5, 0)
else               // a coarse cell: the walk below, then ÷ the cell's voxel count, then to6 once


  
A coarse cell's colour should be the mean colour of every voxel under it. Taken exactly that's 8lod samples a cell: 512 at lod 3, which a
  job can't pay. So the walk goes down meanDepth levels (1 in the engine) and stops. Each sub-cell at that floor counts as
  its voxel count × the field at its centre. Click a cell to see its sub-cells laid out.

  with each step kept, and it's checked against the real cellAttr on every click. The "unweighted" figure is the page's, to show what the weights do.


  
    
    
  • The weights are the children's voxel counts, and the divisor is the cell's. The children's counts add up to the cell's, so it's a true weighted
      mean. A sub-cell with one voxel in it barely moves the colour; a full one counts 8level times. Empty sub-cells aren't visited at all.
    • 
    
  • A sample can land in air. The sample point is the sub-cell's centre, whether or not the voxel there is filled. The table's "there is" column
      says which. This is why §2's fields are defined everywhere, and why a smooth field is forgiving: the centre's colour is close to its neighbours'.
    • 
    
  • It's exact while lod ≤ meanDepth. Then the floor is level 0 and every voxel is sampled with weight 1. Past that, the error is set by the floor
      sub-cell's side against the field's smallest feature. The rows jitter per 4³ block, so depth 1 is exact-looking at lod 3 (4³ sub-cells) and slips at lod 4
      (attrs-findings.md §1). The LOD explainer's §10 shows whole slices of that error.
    • 
    
  • The accumulator is one module-level buffer, ACC, so cellAttr isn't re-entrant: a field that called
      cellAttr itself in the middle of a walk would clobber the sum in progress. Nothing does. The scene field is asked mid-walk and sometimes
      needs a baked child's mean; it calls the child's mean directly, with a buffer of its own.
    • 
    
  • Counts are float64. Exact to level 17, rounded above. That's fine for a weight, and it's why "is this node full?" is asked with
      isFull, never by comparing a count.

06
Baked fields: words by rank


  
An imported asset has no formula for its colour. Its colours come from a texture, sampled once when the mesh is voxelized. So a baked field is
  data: an array of words, and a way to find a voxel's word in it ().

  
    
    
  • The skin is a second bit DAG beside the shape: the voxels that get a word. In the asset pipeline that's every voxel a triangle touches, which
      includes every voxel that can show. Insides have no word: the pack at D = 10 has 2.23M skin voxels of 5.6M occupied.
    • 
    
  • A voxel's rank is its place among the skin's voxels in Morton order, which is the order a depth-first walk of the DAG meets them. Walk down to the
      voxel, add the counts of the octants you pass on the left, and the total is the rank. words[rank] is its colour. No index is stored: the
      skin's own counts are the index.
    • 
    
  • A coarse cell is one subtree, so its skin is one run of ranks [r, r + n). Its mean is that run's sum over n: exact, at
      any lod, with no sampling. This is the mean of what can show, which is the right colour for a coarse cell of an asset.
    •

  • Block sums, not prefix sums. A running sum at every word would be 24 bytes a word (53 MB for the pack at D = 10). Instead there's one every
      BLOCK = 64 words: three float64 a block, 0.4 bytes a word. A range reads two block sums for its whole blocks and scans the ragged ends, at most
      63 words each: 126 words a mean, whatever the lod. Every sum is of integers below 253, so it's exact, and the test compares with ===.
    • 
    
  • Some cells are asked and have no skin. Two kinds. The seam tail: cells only the apron buried, drawn when a neighbouring chunk is at another
      lod; at lod 0 those are inside voxels. And cells a lod rule exposes: a dropped neighbour counts as empty, so a cell of solid inside can show a face. Hover the
      dark middle of the ball to see what they get.
    • 
    
  • The fallback climbs, but never out of its chunk. A cell with no skin takes the mean of its smallest ancestor that has some, at most
      climb = 6 levels up. A level-l cell's level-(l + 6) ancestor is exactly its chunk at lod l. So the word still reads only
      inside its own chunk's box, which is the stamp rule (§7). Past that it takes the whole skin's mean, a constant. Drag climb down to 2 to see that case here; at 6 this small world never reaches it.
    • 
    
  • The path memo. A lod-0 word was a walk of all D levels, and that walk was most of a baked job. But a job asks its cells in nearly Morton order, so
      each walk starts at the deepest level it shares with the last one: the highest bit where the two points differ, by clz32 of their xor. In Morton
      order a skin voxel then reads one or two children instead of D. It's correct in any order, only slower when the asks jump about. Press the button.
    • 
    
  • Six bits in the file. A file can store to6 of each channel and widen it back on read. A lod-0 word can't move: widening lands within
      0.12 of a step of where it started, so to6 gives the same value. A coarse word can move by one step at most. Press the button to count both here.
    • 
    
  • A baked field's lod-0 answer goes through mean too. At level 0 the "mean" of a skin voxel is its own word, and the mean of a
      no-skin voxel is the fallback, unrounded. So one branch serves every lod, and each word is still quantized exactly once.
    •

Edits: ids can't see colour


  
Edit mode places coloured blocks. The shape change goes into the DAG. The colour can't, so it goes into a table beside the field, and the field the
  job uses becomes a wrapper ():

  
editedField = (base, table) => (x, y, z, level) => {
  const c = table.lookup(x, y, z);          // the latest block over this point, or −1
  return c >= 0 ? c : base(x, y, z, level);   // else the world's own field
};

  • The trap. Place a red block: the root changes to some id A. Break it: the root is the id you started with, because hash-consing gives equal
      voxels equal ids. Place a blue block in the same spot: the root is A again. Same voxels, same id, different colour. Anything that decides "is this

    • 
    
  • So a stamp is seven ids and a colour revision. A chunk's job reads seven nodes (its own and its six face neighbours'), and
       records them. Beside them goes the revision of the last op whose block
      overlaps the chunk. An entry is right if its ids match and its revision is at least the chunk's. 
      answers "which revision touches this chunk" without scanning the log: blocks are snapped, so a block and a chunk overlap only if one holds the other.
    • 
    
  • Colour needs only the chunk's own revision, where ids need the neighbours'. A word reads the field only inside its own chunk's box: cell centres,
      or a coarse mean's sub-cells, or a baked climb of at most 6 levels. That's the second half of the stamp rule, and every field on this page keeps it.
    • 
    
  • A break isn't in the table. It removes voxels, and a voxel that's gone has no colour to look up. What it uncovers is the base field's colour,
      which never moved. So a break paints nothing, and the table holds places only.
    • 
    
  • A click that changes no voxel isn't an op. Try placing over solid ground: nothing is logged. So a block placed over solid can't recolour it. That
      was decided (2026-11-05): recolouring is a brush's job, when there is one.
    • 
    
  • The latest block wins, by sequence number. The table is keyed by (level, X, Y, Z) and keeps each block's place in the log. A lookup tries each
      block size in use and takes the hit with the highest sequence. An undo can uncover an older block's colour, which can't be done in place, so an undo
      starts a new epoch and a table seeing a new epoch rebuilds from op 0.
    • 
    
  • Epoch and count, never count alone. Undo then place something else leaves the same count with a different op on the end. A worker comparing
      counts would keep the old colour for that one block.
    • 
    
  • The wrapper has no mean. So an edited baked world would lose its exact means and walk a field that has no words off the skin. No
      baked world can be edited today (files open as views); the edited field has to keep mean before one can.

08
Scenes: a field over placements


  
Dropping a world into a world is the same problem at a larger scale. bakeScene unions the child's voxels into the parent's DAG, and after
  that the DAG can't say whose voxel is whose. A knight dropped where the kit was already solid leaves the kit's node exactly as it was, yet that voxel is
  the knight's now. So a scene's colour is a field over its placements ().
  Asked for the centre of a level-l cell, it does three things:

  
    
    
  • Find the owner: the latest placement whose placed shape has a solid voxel in the cell. Bounds first, then a real test of the child's DAG at its
      offset (boxHits). Bounds alone would be wrong: a parent voxel inside the child's box but outside the child would take the child's colour.
    • 
    
  • Find the child's cell: a level-l parent cell is a level-(l − scale) child cell. Take the one holding the centre, on the child's
      own grid, then go back through the turn. Turns are the 48 cube symmetries and offsets are whole voxels, so cells map to cells exactly.
    • 
    
  • Ask the child: a baked child for that cell's exact skin mean, a named child at that cell's centre. No owner: the parent's field.
    •

Click to drop: the page centres the child's cube on the click, in the slice's plane. (The viewer's dropAt puts it against the face you hit,
  scene-log.md §4.) The picture is the field's answer for each occupied level-l cell of the layer, which is what the coarse walk's floor asks it.
  The slice cuts through the middle of what you drop, so it shows insides the viewer never draws. A baked ball's inside voxels have no word: the blocky colours in its
  middle are §6's fallback, each the mean of the nearest ancestor cell with skin. Its rim is its own words.


  
    
    
  • Why the level is passed. Raise the sample level to 2, then switch "pass the level" off. Without it the field takes the point as one voxel, and
      two things go wrong, both on a dropped child's rim. A cell the child only partly fills fails the owner test whenever the voxel at its centre isn't the
      child's, and takes the parent's colour for a point in air: the pale cells. And where the test does pass, a baked child answers for that one voxel, often
      an inside one with no word, where with the level it gives the exact mean of the whole child cell. This is the one field that reads the fourth argument,
      and anything that wraps it has to pass it on (editedField does).
    • 
    
  • A drop into solid is still an op. Drop a small child deep underground: the root doesn't change at all, and the drop is logged anyway, because the
      colours there are the child's now. A drop with no voxel inside the world isn't.
    • 
    
  • A drop carries T, the child already turned and scaled, as an id in the parent's store. A worker can't intern, so it can't prepare the child
      itself. The new nodes go in the same tail, ahead of the drop that names them. A baked child's words cross once per world, in the first tail after its first drop.
    • 
    
  • Paint is bounds, never shape. A drop repaints every 64-voxel block its content box touches. Over-painting only costs a redone chunk; missing a
      block would leave a wrong colour on screen.
    • 
    
  • The scene field has no mean, and refuses a parent that has one. A field with mean is asked it at every lod, and a named
      parent has none to give. So nothing can be dropped into a baked world yet. With no drops, it hands the parent's field back untouched: undo everything and the bytes are the parent's own.
    • 
    
  • It keeps a short list. The first version tested every placement for every sample: 16 placements cost 1.5–2× a job's colour, nearly all in chunks
      no placement touches. Now it keeps the placements meeting the sample's 64-voxel block and rebuilds the list only when a sample lands in another block:
      1.15–1.5× (scene-log.md §2.2).
    • 
    
  • Blocks and drops in one world are refused. "The latest op wins" would need one log ordering both. Two tables wrapped either way round would each
      be wrong somewhere, so the runner throws instead (scene-plan.md §4 Q3).
    •

10
To the GPU, twice


  
The viewer has two ways to draw a chunk, and each carries words its own way.


  

    

      
Raster: a word per instance

      
A records chunk is one buffer: all the records, then all the words. Two integer attributes read it, one from byte 0 and one from byte 4n, each with
      divisor 1, so every instanced cube gets its record and its word. The vertex shader passes the word on flat, since a cube is one colour.
      

    

    

      
Ray: a run per brick, in a heap

      
A traced chunk has no instances. A ray hits a cell and needs that cell's word. Each 8³ brick with shown cells gets a run: 16 words of
      shown-mask (512 bits, Morton order), then one word per shown cell. A hit's word is at the cell's rank among the set bits, a popcount.

      But whether a cell is shown depends on its neighbours, and its colour depends on where it is. So each page brings its own attribute block.
    • 
    
  • "Shown" means the records plus the seam tail. A ray from outside can only enter an occupied cell through a face whose neighbour is empty, so those
      are the only cells that need a word. A ray that starts inside a solid hits a cell with none, and the shader draws it mid grey (ATTR_NONE).
    • 
    
  • GLSL ES 3.00 has no bitCount, so the rank is a SWAR popcount over at most 16 mask words. GLSL_RAY_ATTR and
      attrAtCPU are line-for-line twins, and a probe holds them equal on every hit.
    • 
    
  • Both paths show the same word for the same cell. ray.test.js draws a view both ways in colour mode 9 (the raw 6-bit channels, unshaded)
      and compares exactly.
    • 
    
  • Words now outweigh occupancy on the ray path. A traced view's attributes are 2–100× its occupancy pages (attrs-findings.md §3).
      Storing only shown cells was still right: the same view as full 8³ tiles would be 628 MB, not 119.

In a file


  
A .vdag file outlives the code that wrote it, and that's a problem for a name: the file says "the terrain's colours", and the
  code that reads it might define those differently by then. So a file stores the descriptor and a print of what it meant when written.

  
    
    
  • The print is a hash of the field at 256 voxels. Each is found by a walk from the root that turns at every level into a present octant the hash
      picks, so every sample lands on a solid voxel, and the walk reads only the graph's shape, never an id. The same world gives the same samples in any store.
      
    • 
    
  • On read, the reader makes the field from the name and prints it again. Equal: same. Different: changed, and the
      viewer warns that the shape is right and the colours may not be. Can't make the field at all: unknown, drawn without.
    • 
    
  • It catches a plan or a palette that moved, not one altered voxel. 256 samples can miss a change confined to a few places. That's the trade.
    • 
    
  • A baked world's colours are in the file, in an ATTR section: the skin as a graph that carries on from the shape's level tables, and
      the words gzipped. Its print is of its own words, and a mismatch there isn't "changed", it's damage: the file is refused.
    • 
    
  • An older reader skips ATTR (readers skip tags they don't know) and opens the world as its shape. A file is outside input: the reader
      checks that the skin is inside the shape, that there's a word per skin voxel, and that nothing is left over.
    •
