Levels of Detail

Far from the eye, the engine draws the world with bigger cubes. That sounds like one idea, but in the code it is five separate decisions, each made in a different place, and each with a trap that took a measurement to find. This page takes them one at a time, with the real code running on real worlds. Wherever a section uses a 2D model instead, it says so.

01

The DAG already is the pyramid

A texture keeps a mip chain: each level is computed from the one below and stored beside it. This engine stores nothing extra for its coarse lods. A node of the DAG at level l is a cube 2l voxels on a side, and it either holds something or it doesn't. So "the world at lod l" is just "the DAG, read down to level l and no further". A coarse cell is a node. A chunk at lod l is 64³ of them, which is one node at level l + 6, so asking for a coarse chunk walks only six levels below its own node.

Slide the lod and watch three things. The cube count falls by about 4× a lod on smooth shapes, because what's drawn is a surface, and a surface has a quarter as many cells each time a cell doubles. It falls by more on solids at first, because more of the inside is buried. And on the gyroid, any (keep a cell if it holds anything) visibly fattens the walls, which is §4's problem.

The tree column is how many cells a coarse lod could draw, before the face test. The DAG column is what is stored: every coarse cell of a level is one of these few nodes. That's why coarse jobs are cheap to ask for, and why §7 can cache a coarse brick's choice per node.

The one thing a coarse lod does need: a count. any only needs "is this node empty?", which the DAG answers for free. Every better rule needs how full a node is, so keeps one float64 per node, summed children-first. A float64 is exact to 253, so a count is exact up to level 17 (817 = 251). Past that, a node missing one voxel has the same count as a full one. That made a job differ from expandChunk once (edit-log.md §2), and it's why exists: the full node of each level is the one whose eight children are all the full node below, and hash-consing makes that an exact test.

02

Where each lod goes: the cut

Start from the top chunk, the whole world at its coarsest lod. Split any chunk that is closer to the eye than K of its own sides, skipping the empty children (the DAG names them). What's left when nothing more splits is the cut. Its leaves partition the world, so every voxel is drawn by exactly one chunk, and each leaf is at the coarsest lod the rule allows at its distance. That's the clipmap of ifsandwhats.md §8.4, built as a cut through an octree rather than as nested boxes.

// selectChunks (stream-core.js), without the heap and the cap
if (c.lod > minLod && c.dist < detail * c.size) c.kids = childrenOf(world, c);   // split: 8 children, minus the empty ones
else leaves.push(c);                                                           // a leaf: drawn at this lod

Chunks per lod (bright: leaves, faint: split above them)

Drag the white eye marker; hover a chunk to see why it stopped splitting. Things to try:

  • Count the chunks per lod. Each lod's ring is twice as far out as the one inside it, and its chunks are twice as big, so in a uniformly filled world every lod would hold about the same number of chunks. Real content spreads them unevenly (empty chunks are never made, and a ring that grazes the ground holds fewer than one full of buildings), but the count per lod stays bounded. So a world 64× bigger costs about one more ring, not 64× more chunks: the cut grows with the log of the world, which is ifsandwhats.md §5.7's "bounded by screen resolution, not world size".
  • Turn K below 1. Red outlines appear: leaves with a face neighbour two or more lods finer. The seam fix (§8) assumes that never happens, and it can't when K ≥ 1 (the proof is below). The viewer's slider stops at 1 for that reason; this one goes lower so you can see it break.
  • Drop maxChunks. Splits are taken biggest-looking first (by dist / size, from a heap), so a cap refuses the splits that would show least. Past the cap, the one-lod promise is off, and red can appear even at K = 2.
  • Raise minLod. Nothing near the eye gets finer than that lod. It's the clamp a weak machine would use; §3 shows its price.

Why face neighbours differ by at most one lod. Say a leaf c of side S had a neighbour two lods finer across a face. That neighbour's parent p has side S/2 and was split, so it is less than K·S/2 from the eye. It lies inside c's face neighbour, within S/2 of c, so c is less than K·S/2 + S/2 from the eye. But c is a leaf, so it is at least K·S away. Both hold only if K < 1. (The first version of this comment said √3, which is the bound for boxes touching at a corner. The test found it slack: seams only meet across faces.)

03

Why distance over size: a cell's size on screen

The split rule compares distance with size, and that is the whole reason it works. A cell of side 2l at distance d spans about 2l/d radians. A leaf stopped splitting because d ≥ K · 64 · 2l, so its cells span at most 1/(64K) radians, whatever its lod. Each point below is a leaf of the cut you just made in §2. Its height is the size of one of its cells on screen, from about the chunk's centre, which is chunkFootprint's side over 64. The thin curves are each lod's cell size as distance grows, and the cut hops from one curve to the next, which gives the sawtooth.

from §2
  • The dashed line is the ceiling, pxPerRadian / (64 (K + ½)): a leaf that could still split would sit above it. Doubling K halves it, and costs about 8× the chunks per ring (a ring K times as far out, in 3D). Points above it are leaves that weren't allowed to split: lod-0 chunks next to the eye (there is no lod −1), anything at minLod, and anything the cap refused.
  • The default isn't a pixel a cell. At K = 2 on a 1080-line screen, cells come out at a few pixels. coverage-findings.md §4 argues that at one pixel a cell, a coarse rule's dither is invisible and only coverage matters; at a few pixels, some of it can show. Worth knowing when you read §4–6's pictures, where the dither is huge on purpose.
  • The same rule picks raster or ray. chooseKinds uses this footprint too (cubes per footprint pixel). Since a leaf's footprint is nearly the same at every lod ≥ 1, that rule mostly asks how many cubes a chunk has (stream-core.js §2b).
04

What a coarse cell is: five rules

The cut says which lod; the next question is what a cell at that lod shows. At lod 0 there's nothing to decide. At lod l a cell holds c of its 8l voxels, and a lod rule decides whether to draw it. Cells are binary, drawn or not, so a coarse rule can only choose which cells to keep. Each of the obvious answers is wrong somewhere (coverage-findings.md):

05

Adaptive: one formula, with its depth measured

The first run showed that volume and opacity are one formula. Suppose a ray through a coarse cell crosses m independent fine samples of density p. It hits one with probability 1 − (1 − p)m, so a brick of n cells should keep

k = n · (1 − (1 − p)m)        m = 1: volume (k = n·p = C / 8^l)     m = 2^l: opacity (every voxel independent)

A sweep of m found no single best value: 1 for solids, up to 6 for porous specimens at lod 3. m behaves like a correlation length, so the rule has to measure it, and the brick already holds the measurement. If its voxels were s independent samples per cell, a cell would be occupied with probability q = 1 − (1 − p)s. The brick knows q (occupied cells over n) and p (voxels over volume), so it can solve for s, and a ray crosses about the cube root of that:

s = log(1 − q) / log(1 − p)     // independent samples per cell
m = clamp(cbrt(s), 1, 2^l)      // adaptiveDepth: q = 1 carries no evidence, so it's read as 2^l
k = min(round(n · (1 − (1 − p)^m)), occupied)

The numbers are keepCount and adaptiveDepth from chunk-core.js, called with a brick of n = 512 cells, C = p · 512 · 8l voxels, and the occupied count from the slider. The slider runs from the fewest cells C voxels can fill (one lump) to the most (scattered).

Drag "occupied cells" from left to right with the density fixed. At the left end the voxels are packed into as few cells as possible, one lump, and m = 1: keep exactly the volume. At the right end the same voxels are spread so every cell gets some, and m = 2l: keep what a ray through independent noise would hit. Everything real is in between, and adaptive reads where. The flat-sheet case is the failure written out: q = 1/8 and p = 1/32 read as about 4 samples a cell, so m ≈ 1.6, and 26 of the sheet's 64 cells survive where face-on the sheet is opaque. The test suite holds that 26 by hand.

07

Why the choice is made per brick

volume, opacity and adaptive can't decide one cell at a time, since they set a budget for a group. The group is the 8³ brick, a node at level l + 3, and that choice of group is load-bearing in three places:

  • The apron sees what the neighbour draws. A brick nests inside a chunk, so the one-cell apron a job reads from its neighbour (to hide faces) is decided by exactly the bricks the neighbour itself decides with. A group that straddled chunks would make two jobs disagree.
  • The choice is a function of the node alone. Count, occupied cells and ties all come from inside the brick's node. So caches a brick's kept cells as a 512-bit mask per (rule, lod, node), and the ray path stores each distinct brick once in its atlas. CLAUDE.md calls this the lod-rule rule: a rule that looked outside its brick would make both caches silently wrong, and ray-core.test.js checks the property.
  • A full cell always survives. If a cell is completely full, k ≥ round(n·p) is at least the number of full cells, and full cells sort first. §8's seam proof needs that.

Inside a brick, ties are common: every cell a flat sheet crosses holds exactly the same count. Breaking them by Morton index would always drop the brick's high corner first. So is a fixed shuffle of the 512 positions, the same in every brick.

08

Seams: where two lods meet

A job hides a cell's face when the cell next to it is occupied, and for a boundary cell it asks the neighbour chunk at the same lod (the apron). But the neighbour may be drawn at another lod, where that cell may not exist, or may be bigger than what it covers. Then a hidden face has nothing in front of it, and you see through the world. A job can't know how its neighbours will be drawn (that changes with every step of the eye), so the fix happens at draw time, in the shader:

// GLSL_DECODE. uOpenSides: one bit per side, set by coverOf when the same-lod neighbour across it isn't drawn.
uint recShown(uint r){ return recMask(r) | (boundaryBits(recCell(r)) & uOpenSides); }

An open side shows every boundary face. That covers faces of cells that have a record; a cell the apron alone buried has none. So each job appends those cells after its records, as the seam tail (mask 0), drawn only when some side is open. And a tail cell is kept only if the coarser cell across its face, the level-(l + 1) node holding the neighbour, isn't completely full.

A 2D teaching model: a side view, two chunks of 32 × 40 voxels, faces drawn as bright lines. A red slit on the seam is a fine stretch where one side is solid, the other isn't, and no face separates them, which in 3D is a hole into an interior with no faces of its own.

Things to try: right half at lod 1 with everything on, then turn each fix off. Without the open side, cracks appear on both sides of the seam, including the coarse side, because its apron saw the fine side's coarse cells, which are fatter than what's drawn. Without the tail, cells buried only by the apron still leave cracks. Turn the full test off and the tail grows (every buried boundary cell joins it) without fixing anything more. Then try lod 2, which the cut never makes when K ≥ 1: the full test looks only one level up, and it can drop a tail cell that was needed.

Why a full coarser cell needs no tail record (stream-plan.md, decision 11). Take a boundary cell n at lod l, and let P be the level-(l + 1) node holding its neighbour. The neighbour chunk is drawn at l − 1 or l + 1 (one lod apart, §2). If it's finer: P full means its children are full, and full cells are kept by every rule (§7). If it's coarser: P itself is the cell drawn, and a full cell is kept. Either way the space across the face is drawn solid, so no crack can open there. Without this test a solid chunk underground carries its whole boundary shell, about 23K records.

09

When lods change: complete beats finer

The cut is what the eye wants. Jobs take time, so what's on screen is whatever is resident, chosen by . Its rule: a region is drawn from its children only if all of them are resident; otherwise its parent stays up. So a region never mixes a chunk with its own descendants (nothing is drawn twice), the picture changes only when a replacement is whole, and the fallback is one lod coarser or finer in normal motion. The top chunk is pinned, so something always covers everything.

Below is the real streamer (createStreamer) running the real job (createJobRunner) on the main thread, on §2's world and eye, so you can throttle it and watch the transitions. In the viewer the same jobs run on workers.

drawn as wanted coarser: a parent standing in finer: children not yet merged hole: nothing to draw open side (seam tail on)
  • Pause the jobs, then drag the eye. Moving closer splits leaves: their children are missing, so the parent stays up (coarser, amber). Moving away merges: the parent is missing, so the children stay (finer, violet). Nothing is ever a hole while the pinned top chunk can stand in.
  • Turn "keep split chunks resident" off. That's the first version's rule, asking only for leaves. With jobs slow, missing leaves fall back past parents that were never loaded, all the way up; in a simulated flight the whole world was drawn at its coarsest for the entire flight (stream-log.md §3). Keeping the split chunks costs about one chunk for every seven leaves.
  • White edges are open sides: the drawn chunk's same-lod neighbour isn't drawn, so its tail shows there (§8). They appear wherever the cover meets a different lod, and only there.
  • Order matters. The queue asks for the pinned chunk, then holes, then everything else nearest-looking first (dist / size), so a region a fallback already covers can wait.
10

A coarse cell's colour

A drawn coarse cell needs a colour. Its word is the mean colour of every voxel under it, weighted by voxel count (), so it doesn't depend on which lod you start from. Taken exactly, that's 8l evaluations of the field a cell, 512 at lod 3, which a job can't afford. So the walk descends meanDepth levels below the cell, and each sub-cell at the floor counts as its voxel count times the field at its centre. The default is 1: eight samples, one per child, weighted by the child's count.

Every word here is the real cellAttr's, on the shape's own colour field. "The exact mean" is cellAttr with meanDepth = lod, which samples every voxel. The "rounded at every lod" figure is this page's: the children's 6-bit words averaged and rounded again, level by level.

  • Depth 0 is a single sample at the cell's centre, and it shows hard switches where the field changes inside a cell (terrain's biome bands). Depth 1 kept every word within one 6-bit step of the exact mean on smooth shapes at lods 1–3 (attrs-findings.md §1).
  • It slips where the field changes inside half a cell: the gyroid's two sides swap within a sub-cell. attrs-findings.md found that at lod 4 on a D = 10 gyroid, and depth 2 fixed it at 2–10× a plain job. This page's gyroid is D = 8, so its sheet is a quarter as many voxels thick, and the slip comes two lods sooner: try it at lods 3 and 4. What matters is the feature against the cell, not the lod.
  • Quantize once. The mean is summed in floats of 8-bit channels and rounded to 6 bits at the end (decision 6). Averaging 6-bit words lod by lod would round at every level; the read-out compares the two.
  • A baked field answers the mean itself. An imported asset's words cover only its skin, so sampling sub-cell centres would read air. Its mean sums the cell's skin as one rank range, exactly, at every lod (baked-core.js).

What this page leaves out

  • Edits across lods. A click changes every node on its path to the root, so it reaches every lod's chunk over it. The streamer tells which entries are still right by their stamp, the seven node ids a job reads (edit-stream-log.md).
  • The ray path's lods. A traced chunk is a page of deduplicated 8³ bricks under the same rules, chosen per chunk by density (the renderer explainer, §8–9).
  • Partial coverage. A binary cell can only match a mean by dithering. Drawing a coarse cell part-transparent is parked with dust (coverage-findings.md, question 3).
