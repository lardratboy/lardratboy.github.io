The Renderer

You fly through a world 65,536 voxels on a side, with 3.45 trillion of them solid, and a frame takes a few milliseconds. The GPU never sees that world. It sees a few hundred 64³ pieces of it, and the rest of the code chooses those pieces, builds them away from the main thread, and never leaves a gap while they arrive. This page shows how the parts fit together first, then takes each part in turn, with the real code running.

01

The whole thing on one page

This is the page's most important section, so start with the viewer's own frame function. Here it is with its details removed:

function frame(){ if (eye moved) streamer.update(eye); // §3 §9 re-cut the world, re-cover it, choose paths, order the job queue pool.dispatch(); // §4 hand jobs to idle workers, and don't wait for them streamer.service(); // §7 admit finished jobs, under a time and a byte budget renderView(); // §10 draw whatever is resident, right now }

Five facts explain almost everything else in the code:

  1. The GPU never sees the DAG. The DAG lives on the main thread, with a copy in each worker. The GPU gets only finished pieces: records or ray pages.
  2. The chunk is the only unit. A chunk is one DAG node holding a 64³ grid of cells. Near the eye a cell is a voxel, and farther away it stands for 2, 4, 8… voxels on a side. Every chunk, near or far, costs about the same to store and draw. That is why a view's cost doesn't grow with the world.
  3. Deciding and doing are separate. Every decision is a pure function in stream-core.js, tested in Node on a virtual clock. The page and stream-pool.js only move messages and buffers. When you read the viewer, you are mostly reading the doing.
  4. Nothing waits. A frame draws what is resident, coarser than wanted if it has to. No frame ever blocks on a job, so flying too fast costs detail. It doesn't cost frame rate, and it doesn't leave holes.
  5. There are two ways to draw a chunk, and one depth buffer joins them. Cubes cost per cube, and rays cost per pixel. Each chunk takes whichever is cheaper, and the ray pass stops at the raster's depth.

What the eye costs, right now, in the world chosen at the top right

Each row is measured live on this page's own streamer, from the eye the other sections share. The drop that matters is from voxels to cubes, by a factor of a thousand or more: that's what chunks and lods buy. The bars use a log scale, or you couldn't see the small ones at all.

The demos below all share one world, one eye and one streamer, with real workers, so what you do in one section shows up in the others. The drawing lab in §7, §9 and §10 is not the viewer. It is a smaller copy of its passes, built on the same GLSL_DECODE and GLSL_RAY and the same cores. §11 lists what it leaves out.

Five sizes, one address

Most of the difficulty in reading the code is keeping these words apart. Level counts up from the voxel: a level-k node is 2k voxels on a side. Lod says how much of that a single cell stands for. Everything else follows from those two, and from always splitting a chunk into the same 64³ cells:

Two details matter later. The address is Morton (octant-major), because the DAG's octant order is Morton, so a depth-first walk emits a chunk's cells already sorted by index. And index >> 9 is the brick, the 8³ block a ray page stores as 512 bits (§8). A brick at lod l is also a real DAG node, at level l + 3, which is what lets two chunks share one.

03

The cut: which chunks the eye wants

The first question has a one-line answer. Start from the top chunk, the whole world at its coarsest. Split any chunk that is closer to the eye than detail of its own sides. Skip the empty children, which the DAG names for free. What's left when nothing more wants to split is the cut: leaves that partition the world, with the split chunks above them. The rule compares distance with size, so a leaf always spans about the same angle on screen, whatever its lod. That is ifsandwhats.md's clipmap, as a cut through an octree.

// selectChunks, without the heap and the cap
if (c.lod > minLod && c.dist < detail * c.size) c.kids = childrenOf(world, c);   // split
else leaves.push(c);                                                           // a leaf of the cut

Drag the white eye marker. Hover over a chunk to see why it stopped splitting, and click one to carry it into §4. Blank areas are empty space, which the cut never visits. Looking from the side shows a terrain best: the cut follows the surface because everything else is empty or buried, and it costs only a few more chunks at each lod.

Each row's world has 64× the volume of the row above, and the cut grows by a fixed ring of chunks per added lod. That is ifsandwhats.md §5.7's "bounded by screen resolution, not world size", and stream-findings.md §1 measures the same thing in bytes: a smooth view costs 4–6 MB from 2,048³ to 4,096³.

The one-lod rule. Face neighbours in the cut differ by at most one lod when K ≥ 1 and the cap doesn't bind. Say a leaf of side S had a neighbour two lods finer. That neighbour's parent, of side S/2, was split, so it is under K·S/2 from the eye. It lies within S/2 of the leaf, so the leaf is under K·S/2 + S/2 away. But as a leaf it is at least K·S away, which needs K < 1. The seam tail (§6) relies on this, and it's why the K slider stops at 1. maxChunks refuses splits in order of dist / size, so a cap costs detail where it shows least, but past the cap the one-lod promise no longer holds.

Everything it does comes down to answering three questions per cell: is it kept, can anything see it, and what does its record say?

What the job produced

The steps, as expandChunk takes them:

  • Walk the node depth-first to the lod's cells, marking each kept cell in a 66³ byte grid. Depth-first in octant order is Morton order, so records come out sorted.
  • Pick which coarse cells to keep. At lod 0 a cell is a voxel, with nothing to decide. At coarser lods a rule looks at each 8³ brick of cells. any keeps every occupied cell, which fills porous content in solid (specimens bloat 1.2–6.8×). adaptive keeps the fullest k cells, with k chosen so the brick blocks about as much light as it did at full detail (coverage-findings.md). Try both on a far chunk of a kit.
  • Walk the apron. One slab of each of the six neighbours at the same lod, so a cell on the boundary knows what's next to it. Without it, solid shapes get 106–225% more faces (expand-findings.md §3). Move the layer slider to −1 or 64 to see an apron slab.
  • Make the masks. For each kept cell, one bit per neighbour that is empty. A cell with all six neighbours occupied shows nothing and gets no record: that's the buried cell, and most of a solid.
  • Keep the tail. A boundary cell the apron alone buried gets a record anyway, after all the others, with a mask of 0. §6 is what it's for.

Why it's 3.5× to 150× smaller than block-showroom's record. A centre takes 12 bytes, and the shader can rebuild it from the cell index, the chunk's origin and the cell size, so the 14-byte record drops to 4. That's the 3.5×, and skipping buried voxels is the rest: 6.7 MB against 441 MB over the shapes corpus (expand-findings.md). A job takes 2–19 ms (median, per world), so 4 workers expand 90–1,500 chunks a second, and the workers are the bottleneck in flight, not the bookkeeping, which takes 0.1–2.5 ms a frame (stream-findings.md).

The shortcut. A chunk that is completely full, with all six neighbours completely full too, expands to nothing, and createJobRunner says so without walking its 262,144 cells. That's most of a terrain's cut underground. It lives in the runner, not in expandChunk, so the dense oracles keep testing the plain code. And ask isFull, never a count: voxel counts are float64, so past level 17 a count can't feel one missing voxel.

05

A draw: one chunk, one instanced call

The chunk from §4, drawn the way the raster pass draws it. There is no vertex data per cube. A chunk is one buffer of records and one drawElementsInstanced of a shared 36-index cube, and the vertex shader builds the rest from the record, gl_VertexID and three uniforms. It's the real GLSL_DECODE from :

// in rasterVS. aRec: this instance's record. A cube has 24 corners, four per face.
uint corner = uint(gl_VertexID), face = corner / 4u;
vec3 p = recCentre(aRec) + CORNER[corner] * uCellSize;   // recCentre = uOrigin + (cell + 0.5) * uCellSize
gl_Position = uViewProj * vec4(p, 1.0);
if (!faceShown(aRec, face)) gl_Position = vec4(2.0, 2.0, 2.0, 1.0);   // a hidden face collapses outside clip space

Things to notice:

  • Every cube sends 12 triangles, whatever it shows. The collapse happens after the vertices are sent, so the draw's cost is per cube, not per face. Wireframe mode shows only what's exposed. In the viewer that's about one in six on a test block (wireframe.md). Per-face instances would send 0.19–0.85× as many triangles at 1.2–5× the bytes, and that hasn't been worth it yet (expand-findings.md §2).
  • The origin is eye-relative. The eye is a double in voxels, and each chunk's uOrigin is (origin − eye) in float32. At 65,536 voxels from the origin, a float32 can only hold steps of 1/128 of a voxel. Near the eye, an eye-relative position is small, so it keeps far finer steps (ifsandwhats.md §2).
  • A chunk's cost on the GPU is its cubes. About 3.4 ns a cube on the Apple GPU benches (path-occupancy.md §1), so 15M cubes is about 50 ms. That's the number that makes the ray path necessary.
06

Seams: why chunks carry a tail

The apron has a catch. A job hides a boundary face because the neighbour at the same lod has a cell there, but the neighbour may be drawn at another lod, where that cell may not be kept. Then nothing covers the hidden face, and you see sky through the world. Records can't fix that at build time, because a job can't know how its neighbours will be drawn (that changes with every step of the eye), and rebuilding a chunk each time would be far too slow. So the choice is made in the shader:

// GLSL_DECODE. uOpenSides: a bit per side, set when the same-lod neighbour across it isn't drawn.
uint recShown(uint r){ return recMask(r) | (boundaryBits(recCell(r)) & uOpenSides); }

A 2D teaching model: one profile, a side view, lod 0 beside lod 0 or lod 1. The bright lines are faces. A red slit is a face the apron hid and nothing replaced.

Showing boundary faces isn't quite enough, because a cell the apron alone buried has no record at all, and needs one. So each job appends those cells after its main records, as the seam tail, with mask 0, and borderStart says where the tail begins. A chunk with no open side draws [0, borderStart), and a chunk with any open side draws everything. The tail is the whole cost of seams on smooth content: 3.6–6.7% of records, or 43% on a dense porous row. In one terrain view, seams were 11K of 402K cubes (stream-findings.md §3).

Why the tail isn't huge. A buried boundary cell joins the tail only if the coarser cell across its boundary (the level-(lod + 1) node holding the neighbour) isn't completely full. A full cell is drawn by every rule at every lod, so no crack can open against it. Without that test, a solid chunk underground would carry its whole boundary shell, about 23K records. The one-lod rule from §3 is what lets the test look just one level up.

07

The streamer: drawing what's here while the rest arrives

The cut says what the eye wants, and the workers take time. The streamer sits between them, and this is the part that's hardest to see from the code, because it's never doing one thing. It holds a cache keyed by lod:x,y,z, and each frame it answers a new question: given what's resident, what should I draw so that every part of the world appears once, as fine as possible? Its rule is complete beats finer: a region is drawn from its children only if all of them are resident. Otherwise its parent stays up.

Where the bytes are

The throttle exists only so you can watch. It caps how many jobs reach the workers a second, and the viewer has nothing like it. Press flush with the throttle at 20 a second to see a world load from the top chunk down.

Four rules to try against the demo

  • The whole cut is wanted, split chunks too (splitsResident), like a texture's mip chain. The first version wanted only leaves, and on a seed-row flight that drew the top chunk alone, the entire world at its coarsest, for 7 frames, with lod gaps of 8. With split chunks resident, that's 0 frames and gaps of 5, for the same number of jobs and 14–25% more bytes (stream-findings.md §4). Set Wants to "leaves only", throttle, flush, and watch how far a missing chunk falls back.
  • Job order is a priority, not a list. The pinned top chunk first, because it guarantees something can always be drawn. Then holes. Then everything else by dist / size, which loads coarse to fine, so a chunk's parent is normally in before it is. Kind switches go last: they already draw correctly, so a switch only makes them cheaper.
  • Admission has a budget, and the first one always goes. Each frame, service() takes finished results, nearest first, while under installMs (1 ms) and uploadBytes (8 MB). An oversize result can't stall the queue, because the first install is free. That's why results can queue up even with fast workers.
  • Eviction is LRU, and never touches what's wanted. Nothing in the cut, nothing drawn, not the top chunk. So a cache can exceed its budget by exactly the working set, and a 1-byte cache is legal. It degrades instead of breaking: 0 holes, but 1.3× the jobs on a seed row and 8.9× on a terrain flight (stream-findings.md §5). Pick "1 byte" and fly in a circle.

Where edits fit in. A click makes a new root in the same store. setRoot doesn't flush anything. It opens a transaction: a second cut of the new root, and jobs only for what that cut would draw wrongly, 2 to 14 chunks on the worlds measured. Their results are held until the last one lands, and then they all install in one frame, the flip, so every frame draws one root. What lets the cache keep everything else is the stamp: a chunk's job reads exactly seven nodes (its own and six neighbours), and equal ids mean equal voxels, so an entry built from the same seven ids is still right, whatever else changed. edit-stream-log.md has the rest. The "Follow a click" tour in §1 walks it through the map.

08

Rays: the other way to draw a chunk

Cubes cost per cube. A dense porous row is 15M cubes and 103 MB in a single view. But the same row is a DAG of under 250 nodes, so its content has very few distinct pieces. The ray path stores a chunk as a page and walks it per pixel. The cost moves from cubes to pixels, and the storage can share pieces between chunks.

A brick: 16 words, 32 cells a word

Why Morton makes empty blocks free. Cells in a brick are numbered in Morton order, so an aligned 2³ block is one byte of a word, and an aligned 4³ block is two whole words. Asking "is this block empty?" is a mask, not a loop. So classify always returns the largest empty aligned block it can see: an empty octant of the tree, a raster chunk, an empty brick, a 4³, a 2³, or a single cell. That took the sparse far view from about 450 texture reads a ray to about 124 (ray-findings.md). The tracer also remembers its current page, skipping the walk from the root while it stays inside.

// the march, ray-glsl.js's traceRay and ray-core.js's traceCPU, line for line
while (inside the world && t < tEnd) {
  size = classify(cell);              // 0: occupied, a hit. Otherwise the side of an empty aligned block.
  if (size == 0) return hit(t, cell, axis);
  lo   = cell - cell % size;          // that block's corner
  t    = exit of the box [lo, lo + size];
  cell = the integer cell across that face;   // carried, never re-derived from the rounded point
}

Why the cell is carried. Re-deriving a cell from floor(o + t·d) looks equivalent, but after rounding the point can land back in the block it just left, and the ray stalls until its budget runs out. At radix 4 that would have truncated 34% of rays at L = 7 (tracer-cell-carry.md). Colour: a brick is shared, but a cell being shown depends on its neighbours, so a word can't live in the brick. A page has its own run per brick: the shown mask, then one word per shown cell. A hit finds its word by rank, a popcount of the mask below it, in SWAR because GLSL ES 3.00 has no bitCount.

09

The path: raster or ray, chunk by chunk

Neither path wins everywhere, so each chunk is priced. Raster costs cubes · c_cube. A ray costs footprint · c_px, the pixels its box covers on screen, and a ray stops sooner in a fuller chunk. Dividing gives one number and one line. A chunk is traced when its cubes per footprint pixel exceed a threshold that falls as the chunk fills, from 0.27 when empty to 0.025 when full, fitted to the real-GPU benches. Both inputs come from the job's own report: recordCount and occupied. That's why a chunk has to be built once before it can be priced. Until then it takes its parent's kind.

And the rule is written three times, in stream-core.js, the test's expectedKinds and the page's invariants(), on purpose.

10

A frame, pass by pass

Everything above produces a draw list, and turns it into pixels. This lab really runs those passes, on the world chosen at the top, and each tab shows what's in the framebuffer after a stage. The rule that makes the two paths compose: the cover is a partition, so they never draw the same voxel, and the raster goes first so its depth can bound the rays.

the lab draws where you're looking
  1. Raster, into an offscreen target. Near chunks first, so the depth test rejects as much as it can. A frustum test drops whole chunks. Traced chunks are skipped here, and read as empty space to it.
  2. Read the depth as a texture. The depth attachment is a texture, so the next stages can sample it. That's why the canvas has no MSAA: a blit can't write into a multisampled framebuffer, and a resolved depth would put a dark fringe around raster silhouettes.
  3. Mark the traced chunks in a stencil. Each traced chunk's box, grown by one cell, is drawn with no culling. Its fragment shader finds where the pixel's ray enters the box and discards if the raster's surface comes first.
  4. The ray pass, on marked pixels only. One full-screen triangle, stencil-tested. Each ray marches from the near plane to the raster's surface, so a hit is always nearer than the raster: a hit simply overwrites, with no depth test and no gl_FragDepth.
  5. Blit to the canvas. The colour renderbuffer holds both paths by now.

T.fb: the raster pass draws here

COLOR0: a renderbuffer, shared DEPTH: a texture, DEPTH_COMPONENT24

T.rayFb: the marking and ray passes draw here

COLOR0: the same renderbuffer STENCIL: its own renderbuffer. No depth at all.

Why two framebuffers. WebGL2 wants depth and stencil in one image when both are attached, and that image would be the depth texture the ray pass samples. Sampling a texture attached to the framebuffer being drawn is a feedback loop, an INVALID_OPERATION whatever the depth mask says. So the ray framebuffer has no depth, and the hand-written depth test in the box shader takes its place (stencil-pass.md §1).

Three traps that cost real milliseconds

  • Unbounded rays. Before the bound, every pixel marched its whole ray, covered or not. On one terrain view, 14 far chunks cost 5.4 ms, more than half of tracing all 705 (ray-findings.md). Open the ray steps tab and turn the bound off. Raster content is empty to a ray, so unbounded rays fly straight through it.
  • A full-screen pass. With 82 traced chunks mostly hidden under a terrain, the pass cost 5.32 ms a frame across the whole screen, and 1.53 ms with the stencil, where the pass itself is about 0.15 ms (stencil-pass.md §8).
  • discard. The first ray pass discarded a miss, and on Safari that defeated the stencil: Metal runs a fragment function that can discard before the depth and stencil tests unless it's marked for early tests, which WebGL2 can't ask for. Same cut, same 1% mask: 6.07 ms with discard, 2.86 without. So a miss now writes transparent black and blends (stencil-pass.md §7).

Occlusion: skipping what's behind a wall

After the raster pass, each raster chunk's box, grown by a cell, is drawn inside an occlusion query, depth-tested with every write off. If no sample passes, later frames skip its cubes and keep asking. It doesn't change a frame at rest: occlusion.test.js holds the frame to the one drawn without, byte for byte. A cube inside the grown box is at least a cell behind the box's front faces, so wherever the cube would win the depth test, the box would have passed first. The price is a late answer: a query can't return before the page goes back to the event loop, so a chunk revealed by a move can be missing for a frame or two. In the caves' start chamber it skips 93% of the cubes at D = 10, and takes the frame from 2.63 to 1.57 ms at D = 12. On an open view it's pure cost, so only the caves and the kit default to it. What this page's lab leaves out

  • Its passes are a shorter copy of renderView, with the same order, targets and uniforms. GLSL_DECODE, GLSL_ATTR, GLSL_RAY and GLSL_RAY_ATTR are imported, not copied; the raster, box and ray-pass wrappers around them are copied, so a later change to the viewer's passes won't reach this page. It adds a steps-heat mode and a bound switch for §10.
  • No depth readback, no wireframe in the lab (§5 has one), no edit mode, no gamepad, no bench, no ?raymiss=discard. Its texture pools are smaller than the viewer's, so a ray-only view of a dense row may run out of attribute heap and draw grey, as the viewer's HUD warns.
  • §6's seam picture and the bench table are what they say: a 2D model, and numbers copied from the docs. Everything else is measured here, on your machine, with the repo's own cores.
